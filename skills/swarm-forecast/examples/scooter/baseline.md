# Scenario Report: Stakeholder Reactions to Harborview Sidewalk Ban Announcement

In the first 72 hours following the March 3rd announcement, stakeholder reactions crystallize around a critical gap between the city's mandate and its infrastructure commitments, shifting discourse from safety justification to implementation feasibility.

## Finding 1: Courier Association activates grievance through exclusion narrative [inferred]

The Harborview Couriers' Association, representing about 6,000 riders, was "not consulted" prior to the announcement. This absence of consultation becomes the primary framing device in the Association's response. The group's explicit warning that "riders will not risk road traffic without protected lanes" redefines the debate: rather than accepting the ban as a public safety necessity justified by 212 pedestrian injuries, the Association frames it as an unfeasible mandate that ignores rider safety and livelihoods. This messaging reverberates through courier networks in days one and two, establishing the Association as a counterweight to municipal authority.

## Finding 2: The unfunded-lanes framing rapidly displaces the safety rationale [seed + inferred]

Within the first three days, the question of infrastructure funding surfaces and becomes the dominant interpretive frame. The Harborview Courier reports that "two councillors had privately questioned the 40 km lane promise as unfunded"—a detail that transforms technical delivery into political vulnerability. The city promised 40 km of new protected lanes "within two years," but councillors' private doubts about funding become public knowledge through newspaper reporting. This unfunded-lanes framing captures a core tension: the city has imposed a requirement without demonstrating financial commitment to its own infrastructure obligation. Discourse pivots from whether the ban is justified to whether it is implementable, and implementation credibility rests on funding that appears uncertain.

## Finding 3: Platform compliance masks economic pressure redistribution [seed + inferred]

Quick-Bite, the largest food-delivery platform, states it "will comply" and is "reviewing delivery fees." The platform's public posture signals cooperation with municipal authority, but the phrase "reviewing delivery fees" signals internal cost restructuring. Given that riders must now use road lanes rather than faster sidewalks, and protected lanes remain unfunded, delivery economics are likely to deteriorate. The platform's compliance deflects responsibility; riders absorb the operational and safety costs of the ban's infrastructure gap. This dynamic—platform cooperation coupled with fee review—reaches driver networks within 72 hours and likely fuels concerns about livelihood sustainability.

## Finding 4: Safety advocacy persists but loses primacy [seed + inferred]

Greywalkers "welcomed" the ban and called for "faster enforcement," representing the constituency directly harmed by current practices. However, in the first three days, this voice does not expand the conversation; rather, it is eclipsed by infrastructure politics. The 212 pedestrian injuries that justified the ban remain on record, but they are no longer the primary frame for stakeholder discourse. Greywalkers' advocacy provides moral authority for the ban, but the unfunded-lanes framing redirects attention to whether the city can deliver its side of the bargain.

## Synthesis

Yes, the unfunded-lanes framing takes over. By day three, the central storyline is not pedestrian safety or rider obligation, but municipal credibility. The Couriers' Association's non-consultation establishes grievance; councillors' public doubt about funding amplifies implementation skepticism; and Quick-Bite's compliance coupled with fee review signals cost redistribution to riders. The gap between promised infrastructure and municipal funding becomes the organizing principle of stakeholder reactions, overshadowing both the safety rationale and riders' livelihoods concerns.
